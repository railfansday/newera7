The 6 Best Miniseries Released Between 2010 and Now

The best thing about a miniseries is that nobody has to stretch the plot to fill a third season. There’s no “it gets good in season two,” so no one has to wait a year to find out if the show got better. You watch it, it ends, and you can tell a friend to go see it without adding a list of warnings.

These six are the ones I’d push on someone without thinking twice. A few of them will make you cry, one will probably make you check your phone a little less, and none of them waste a single hour of your time. They also happen to be some of the best-acted shows of the last fifteen years.

Jonathan Pine (Tom Hiddleston), a former soldier, works as a hotel night manager until he’s recruited to get close to the arms dealer Richard Roper (Hugh Laurie). Jed (Elizabeth Debicki), Roper’s girlfriend, becomes a key piece of the plan, and Corky (Tom Hollander), Roper’s loud, sweaty right-hand man, steals nearly every scene he’s in. Susanne Bier directed all six episodes of The Night Manager, which is rare for a series this size.

Hiddleston won a Golden Globe for the role, and so did Laurie and Olivia Colman, who plays the intelligence officer chasing Roper. The show also set off a wave of speculation that Hiddleston would be the next James Bond, since he looked so at ease in a tuxedo. Bier won an Emmy for her directing, and the series still is the best modern version of a John le Carré story.

Camille Preaker (Amy Adams), a newspaper reporter in St. Louis, goes back to her small hometown in Missouri to cover the murders of two young girls. She stays with her controlling mother, Adora (Patricia Clarkson), and her teenage half-sister, Amma (Eliza Scanlen), and old wounds open up fast. Sharp Objects comes from Gillian Flynn’s first novel, a few years before she wrote Gone Girl, and Jean-Marc Vallée directed all eight episodes.

Camille has carved words into her own skin, and the show hints at this by flashing single words on screen in the background, on posters and signs, as if they’re surfacing in her head. Vallée cuts quickly between the present and her memories, so the story always feels slightly off balance. Clarkson won a Golden Globe for her role, and Adams, who also produced, delivers one of her best performances.

Craig Mazin, who wrote the series, was mostly known for comedies like The Hangover Part II and Scary Movie 3 before this, which surprised a lot of people. Chernobyl follows the 1986 nuclear disaster through scientist Valery Legasov (Jared Harris), and through ordinary people like Lyudmilla Ignatenko (Jessie Buckley), whose husband is a firefighter sent to the burning reactor without proper protection.

Composer Hildur Guðnadóttir built the score from sounds she recorded at an actual nuclear power plant, the rumble of machinery, the hum of the walls, and it’s a big part of why the show feels so tense. The cast speaks English with British accents instead of Russian ones, a choice that bothered a few viewers but made the show easier to follow for others. It won ten Emmys, including Outstanding Limited Series.

Four Netflix characters, four completely different ways of showing up for somebody. This is the one who fits how you actually do friendship, and what having them in your life would be like.

You do not do friendship at half volume, and neither does he. What you want is somebody who says yes to the plan before you have finished describing the plan.

You are the friend who makes things happen, and so is she. Together you would be mildly exhausting to everyone around you and completely delighted with yourselves.

You have no patience for pleasant lies, and she has no ability to tell them. What looks like coldness from the outside is the most reliable friendship on this list.

You look after whoever is having the worst day. So does he. It is the least strategic way to move through the world and the best possible quality in a friend.

Arabella (Michaela Coel) is a young writer in London who goes out for a night, gets her drink spiked, and slowly pieces together that she was sexually assaulted. Her friends Terry (Weruche Opia) and Kwame (Paapa Essiedu) deal with their own troubles, and the show talks about consent in friendships, dating, and social media. Coel created, wrote, co-directed, and starred in I May Destroy You, drawing on her own experience.

She reportedly wrote 191 drafts of the script. When Netflix wanted to buy the project and own the rights, she walked away, and HBO and the BBC picked it up instead, letting her keep control. Coel won an Emmy for writing, and she’s spoken about how important that decision was. It’s funny, painful, and honest about how messy recovery can be.

Mare Sheehan (Kate Winslet) is a detective in a small Pennsylvania town where everyone knows her as the high school basketball star who won the state championship in 1989. A young mother is found murdered, and Mare juggles the case with her family and a young county detective, Colin Zabel (Evan Peters), assigned to help her. Guy Pearce plays Richard Ryan, a writer who moves into Mare’s orbit.

The show is full of local details, from Wawa coffee and hoagies to the way people say “wooder” for water. Winslet won an Emmy, and so did Julianne Nicholson and Jean Smart, who played her friend Lori and her mother, Helen. Winslet reportedly insisted that HBO not airbrush her in the promotional photos, and she looks like a real woman on screen. That’s part of why the series feels so believable.

Jamie Miller (Owen Cooper), a thirteen-year-old boy, is arrested for killing a girl at his school, and his father, Eddie (Stephen Graham), tries to understand how it happened. Graham co-created Adolescence with writer Jack Thorne, and each of its four episodes is filmed in one unbroken take, planned over months of rehearsal.

The third episode, in which a psychologist (Erin Doherty) talks with Jamie, is the one most people remember, since the whole hour is one tense conversation. Cooper was only about fourteen during filming and later became the youngest male actor to win an Emmy in an acting category. The show sparked a national conversation in the UK, reaching Parliament, and Netflix made it available to schools. It runs just four hours, and almost nobody forgets it.
